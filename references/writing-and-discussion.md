# Response writing, chair communication, and follow-ups

## Supported prose without overcommitment

Start with the answer. Use these patterns only when their premises are established:

- **Factual clarification:** “The method uses A, rather than B. The submitted algorithm specifies [verified detail]. We will make this distinction explicit in [proposed edit].”
- **Evidence-backed disagreement:** “The requested comparison assumes X, whereas our evaluation fixes Y. The relevant comparison is Z because [scientific reason]. [Verified evidence or an honest account of what is still missing].”
- **Bounded concession:** “The evidence establishes X under Y; it does not establish Z. We will narrow [claim] accordingly.” Then check whether this preserves a meaningful contribution.
- **Completed experiment:** “Using [matched conditions], we measured [supplied result with appropriate uncertainty]. This supports [specific inference].” Do not infer significance from the adjective “better.”
- **Incomplete experiment:** “We do not yet have evidence for X. The submitted results address Y, and leave Z unresolved.” In author notes, give a feasible plan if requested.

If asked for a provisional draft, use an unmistakable missing-evidence note outside the submission text, or supply explicitly conditional alternative sentences. Do not write “accuracy [TBD], outperforming baseline” before the direction is known. An asterisk does not make a speculative result acceptable.

Use thanks sparingly and sincerely. Neither apologies nor disagreement phrases are mandatory tokens. Admit technical errors accurately. Avoid suggesting the reader is careless, unintelligent, or unqualified. Preserve negative results that change the claim, even when their inclusion weakens the case.

## Allocation under limits

Select response units by decision relevance and evidence density. Internally account for all substantive concerns; final prose need not spend equal words on every item. For merged concerns preserve reviewer IDs and differences. For separate threads use short local answers with enough setup to be understandable. Include an opening strengths summary only when it adds decision context and attribution is accurate.

Do not cut controls, uncertainty, or a necessary concession to preserve promotional sentences. References, headings and captions may count toward limits; use the actual rule. Never label a text estimate as a verified one-page PDF. Keep revisions within permitted manuscript scope.

## Two distinct chair outputs

### Technical decision summary

Useful when substantive disagreement remains and the mechanism permits a summary. State: disputed claim, review positions accurately paraphrased, decisive evidence, what remains unresolved, and any proposed scope correction. Its purpose is synthesis, not a complaint. It need not accuse anyone of a process failure to be useful.

If an author requested an unsupported accusation, explain the channel change in author notes. The actual technical summary should omit the rejected accusation, demands to discard scores, and denials such as “we are not asking you to discard the score.” Include score history only when it adds necessary procedural context; otherwise keep attention on the substantive disagreement.

### Process or review-quality report

Use the venue's category, timing, recipients, and required evidence. Give a short exact discrepancy or policy issue, the verified manuscript/rule location, and its material consequence. Request consideration or the prescribed remedy rather than choosing the decision for the chair.

Schematic structure: “We report [specific issue] under [applicable category, if any]. The review states [faithful short excerpt]; [verified evidence] shows [discrepancy]. This affects [evaluation point]. Our response addresses the substantive question at [actual response location]. We ask that this evidence be considered.”

A tone complaint, a low score, or nonresponse does not automatically establish a violation. Conversely, do not invent a universal prohibition on reporting silence if the venue explicitly provides such a category. Do not require an author to keep debating when a prescribed report is the appropriate channel. Separate technical replies from private reports and verify intended readers before any authorized submission.

## Discussion and rehearsal

On each follow-up record what was actually said, what changed, the concern ID, and which reply slot remains. Do not treat “I will consider raising” as an observed score change. Answer a new objection substantively before deciding it is improper scope expansion. Only characterize it as a policy violation if the applicable rules support that claim.

When a reviewer is satisfied, a short acknowledgement may suffice; it is not always worth consuming a limited reply. When silent, keep the status unknown and avoid speculative diagnoses. Distinguish a first purposeful invitation to discuss an unresolved issue from repeated requests for engagement or score changes.

### Initiate discussion while there is time to act

Establish when reviewers can actually see the initial response, when two-way discussion closes, and whether unsolicited follow-ups and chair contact are allowed. Count elapsed time from that visibility/opening point, not from an earlier author upload. Check notices and actual reply limits before recommending contact.

For a seven-day open discussion with no acknowledgement, a reasonable planning example is one short reviewer follow-up around day 3 and, if silence persists, a request for chair facilitation around day 5. These are adjustable suggestions, not conference rules or mandatory deadlines. Adapt to the window length, remaining work, existing engagement, and any organizer instructions discouraging reminders. A single-response mechanism does not acquire extra reply slots through this advice.

A reviewer follow-up should identify one or two unresolved decision-critical questions, point briefly to the evidence already provided, and invite specific residual concerns while time remains. New experiments are not a prerequisite for asking this. Do not repeat the full rebuttal, demand a score change, assert satisfaction, or imply that silence grants approval.

Example: “We would welcome clarification on any remaining concerns about the controlled comparison and evaluation checks in our response, so that we can address them while the discussion remains open.” Tailor the objects to the actual review; omit generic reminders when the reviewer is already engaged.

If important engagement remains absent and the venue permits it, ask the chair to help identify or facilitate discussion of the outstanding questions. State the observed lack of author-visible follow-up and time remaining, without claiming to know internal activity or alleging misconduct. Escalate to a process report only when the specific policy and evidence warrant it. Responsibility for a functioning discussion is shared with reviewers and chairs; the author's failure to send a reminder does not excuse an established process failure.

If self-review reveals an omitted factual answer, correct it directly through an allowed channel rather than waiting to be asked again. Before closing, prepare a concise technical synthesis of supported claims and remaining issues if useful and permitted. Drafting a follow-up does not authorize sending it, creating an automation, or repeatedly monitoring a forum.

### Interpret retrospective records carefully

Separate comment creation time, edit time, release/visibility time, and the author's last opportunity to reply. A final justification worded as a request may reveal an unclosed question, but does not establish that it was visible during author discussion. Without history, do not blame the author for ignoring a late-visible request or infer deliberate reviewer delay. An unused discussion opportunity may have helped clarify evidence; it does not establish that acceptance would have followed.

For an independent read, provide the manuscript excerpts, reviews, proposed response, and applicable rules. Ask which concerns remain unanswered, which factual statements lack support, whether a concession changes the central contribution, and what follow-up would expose a weakness. Do not provide a desired verdict or ask the agent to impersonate a real reviewer’s psychology. Verify each criticism against source material. Record a manual audit as such; do not describe it as an independent multi-agent test.
